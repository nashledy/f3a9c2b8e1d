01 The Thesis

Fiction taught us to fear the helix. It's time to outgrow the story.

Jurassic Park. Blade Runner. Gattaca. A century of audiences trained to associate genetic engineering with catastrophe.

But CRISPR is not a screenplay. It's a working tool—built from the oldest immune system on earth—that edits single letters of DNA. While legislators debate dystopias that may never arrive, real patients wait for treatments the science can already deliver.

The position of this brief: regulation should enable innovation, not suppress it. Open. Adaptive. Globally coordinated. Anything less is fear pretending to be policy.

01 / Openness

Trust, earned by consistency.

What It Actually Is

A bacterial immune system, repurposed as a scalpel.

Bacteria invented CRISPR. They use it to cut up viral DNA. Doudna and Charpentier figured out how to redirect it—at any letter we want.

A guide RNA leads the Cas9 enzyme to a chosen target. Cas9 makes a precise cut. The sequence can then be removed, repaired, or replaced.

That's the tool. The debate is what we let it do.

A Brief Chronology

From bacterial defense to Nobel Prize.

1987
Strange repeats discovered
Researchers spot odd repeating sequences in E. coli. No one knows what they're for.
2007
An immune system identified
Bacteria, it turns out, were using these sequences to cut up viruses.
2012
Doudna & Charpentier repurpose it
Programmable. Targetable. The genome becomes editable.
